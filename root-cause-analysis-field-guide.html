Root Cause
Analysis

How to move from a messy symptom to defensible causal claims, proportionate action and evidence that the change actually worked.

The governing question: what combination of conditions made this outcome possible, how confident are we, and what change will reduce recurrence without creating a different problem?

01 / PURPOSE

Investigate the mechanism—not merely the visible failure

Root cause analysis is a structured way to learn from an unwanted outcome. It reconstructs what happened, tests why it happened, and converts the strongest supported explanations into changes that can be checked in practice.

What you notice

Symptom

The place where the problem became visible: a missed appointment, defective component, failed deployment or sudden fall in retention.

What happened first

Trigger

The event that starts the path to the outcome. A trigger matters, but it may cause harm only when other conditions are present.

What helped produce it

Causal factor

An evidence-supported condition that changed the probability, severity, detection or recovery of the outcome.

What changes next

Corrective action

A controlled change linked to a causal mechanism, with an owner, measures, guardrails and a review date.

“Root cause” is convenient language, not a promise that every problem has one deepest cause. In simple failures, a short causal chain may be sufficient. In complex services and sociotechnical systems, the honest result is often a network of interacting conditions.
Restore

Incident response

Stabilise first. Protect people, service, data and evidence. A workaround can be correct now even though it does not explain the event.

Understand

Diagnosis and RCA

Reconstruct the event, distinguish trigger from mechanism, test rival explanations and identify where the system can be changed.

Improve

Continuous improvement

Use PDSA, A3, Lean or DMAIC when the main job is closing a measured performance gap rather than explaining one discrete incident.

Create

Design and discovery

Use research, prototyping and experiments when the problem is ambiguous and a new product, service or experience is needed.

Decide

Decision analysis

Compare options under uncertainty using explicit criteria, scenarios, sensitivity analysis and the value of additional information.

Prevent

Prospective risk analysis

Use FMEA, bow-tie, HAZOP or STPA to examine possible future failure paths. These are forward-looking methods, not retrospective RCA.

A symptom is where the problem became visible. It is not necessarily where the problem began.Working principle for every investigation
02 / CAUSAL THINKING

A plausible story is not yet an explanation

Good RCA makes uncertainty visible. Evidence must be able to strengthen, weaken or overturn the team’s first story—even when that story came from an experienced person.

Replace this

“Who caused it?”

Human error is normally a starting clue. Ask what information, workload, interface, incentives, constraints, training and trade-offs shaped the action at the time.

With this

“How did the system permit it?”

Study controls, feedback, dependencies and recovery paths. Ask why the action made sense locally and why another part of the system did not detect or absorb it.

Temporal order

The proposed cause must occur before the outcome. A later observation may be a consequence rather than a cause.

Mechanism

Explain the connecting process: how could this condition change what happened? Naming an association is not enough.

Contrast

Compare failed and successful cases, before and after, locations, shifts, versions or exposed and unexposed groups.

Rival explanations

Write down alternatives and what evidence would distinguish them. Do not quietly discard inconvenient possibilities.

Counterfactual

Ask whether the outcome would probably have changed if the factor were different, then state the assumptions behind that judgement.

Confidence

Label conclusions as tentative, supported or strong. Confidence should reflect evidence quality, not the seniority of the speaker.

Two stopping errors: stopping at the first plausible cause produces shallow actions; drilling forever produces unusable analysis. Stop when the important causal claims are supported well enough to act, rival explanations have been considered, and effectiveness can be measured.
03 / PROCESS

The evidence-to-learning loop

The deliverable is not a fishbone, timeline or signed report. It is a tested change that reduces recurrence, consequence, or time to detect and recover.

Contain and make safe

Output: the situation is stabilised, temporary controls are recorded and volatile evidence is preserved. Restoration and explanation remain separate jobs.

Frame the problem

Output: a neutral statement of expected versus actual, where, when, scale, impact, trend and boundary—with no presumed cause embedded in it.

Set scope and team

Output: a proportionate response, a clear investigation question and a team including people who know, do and experience the work.

Build the factual record

Output: a timestamped event-and-conditions timeline that distinguishes observations, records, recollections, assumptions, contradictions and gaps.

Map the work system

Output: people, tasks, tools, environment, policies, interfaces, dependencies, controls and feedback—plus differences between work-as-imagined and work-as-done.

Generate hypotheses

Output: several testable explanations covering what changed, which barriers weakened, why detection lagged and what amplified or limited the effect.

Test causal factors

Output: supported and rejected claims, rival explanations, evidence provenance and confidence. Triangulate sources; reproduce only where safe.

Design actions

Output: changes connected to causal mechanisms. Prefer elimination, simplification, constraints and resilient design over reminders or retraining alone.

Implement with ownership

Output: owner, due date, resources, measurable end state, risk check and review route for every action.

Verify and share learning

Output: evidence of implementation and effectiveness after sufficient exposure, guardrail results, updated standards and a rule for reopening the analysis.

04 / WORKED CASE

Follow one question from signal to verified action

This fictional NHS-style teaching case shows the reasoning, not a claim about any real organisation or patient group. The figures are deliberately simple so the causal discipline remains visible.

Governing question

Why did missed outpatient appointments rise after the booking-system change, which conditions materially contributed, and what should change without worsening access or staff workload?

The question names the observed outcome, the suspected time boundary, the need to test more than one condition and the guardrails that prevent a narrow “fix”.

Before change8.1% missed

810 of 10,000 scheduled appointments in the preceding comparison period.

After change12.7% missed

1,270 of 10,000 scheduled appointments after the new booking process stabilised.

Impact+460 misses

A real investigation would add care delay, unused capacity, cost, equity and patient-experience measures.

Neutral problem statement

Expected versus actual

Across the included outpatient clinics, the missed-appointment rate increased from 8.1% to 12.7% after the booking-system change. The investigation covers booking, reminders and rescheduling from referral to appointment date. It does not assume the software caused the increase.

Initial hypotheses

What could explain the change?

H1: reminder delivery weakened during data migration. H2: appointment lead times increased. H3: patients had fewer workable ways to reschedule. H4: clinic or patient mix changed. H5: the apparent rise came from a definition or denominator change.

These are investigation routes, not findings.

Reminder eligibility was damaged during migration

The rise was concentrated where reminders were not successfully attempted. Mapping checks showed that some valid contact records no longer met the new eligibility rule. Send logs supplied the mechanism and timing.

Strong support

Long lead time amplified the effect

Appointments booked more than six weeks ahead had higher missed rates before and after the change; their share increased after implementation. This is a contributing condition, not evidence that waiting alone caused every miss.

Supported

The recovery route weakened

The self-service reschedule route disappeared and abandoned calls increased. Walkthroughs and patient accounts showed that some people tried but could not release or move the appointment.

Supported

Two attractive rivals became less likely

The operational definition and denominator were unchanged, and the clinic mix was broadly stable. That weakens measurement and mix explanations, but the report should state residual uncertainty rather than declare them impossible.

Rivals weakened
Action package

Change the mechanisms

  • Repair contact-data mapping and validate reminder eligibility during migration.
  • Create a visible failed-reminder queue with ownership and recovery rules.
  • Restore an accessible rescheduling route across digital and telephone channels.
  • Reduce excessive lead time where capacity and clinical priority allow.
  • Test changes with patients and booking staff before full rollout.
Verification plan

Prove more than implementation

  • Primary: missed-appointment rate by clinic and patient group.
  • Process: reminder eligibility, delivery and reschedule completion.
  • Guardrails: waiting time, abandoned calls, staff workload and access inequalities.
  • Exposure: review after eight weeks and enough appointments for a stable comparison.
  • Reopen: investigate again if the total improves but a subgroup worsens.
Why this is RCA rather than storytelling: the analysis began with several hypotheses, used contrasts and mechanisms, retained uncertainty, linked actions to supported causal paths and defined what evidence would show that the response worked.
05 / CHOOSE

Use the lightest method that can answer the question

A method is a lens. Select it for the structure of the problem and the evidence available—not because the organisation has always used it.

Three questions before choosing

Has the event already happened? If not, use prospective risk analysis. Is the path simple or interactive? A short chain needs less machinery than a complex service. Do you have one event or repeated process data? A timeline explains sequence; process mining examines many recorded journeys.

Clear sequence

Timeline + 5 Whys

AnswersWhat happened in what order, and what conditions connect each step?

Switch whenThe chain branches, evidence conflicts, or “why” repeatedly ends at a person.

Broad contributors

Fishbone + evidence matrix

AnswersWhat categories of explanation should be tested?

Switch whenInteractions or controls matter more than a category list.

Known boundary

Change analysis

AnswersWhat differs between before and after, failed and successful, site, shift or version?

Switch whenMany changes occurred together and causal separation is weak.

Controls expected

Barrier / bow-tie analysis

AnswersWhich preventive, detective, mitigating or recovery controls were absent, weak or bypassed?

Switch whenThe control structure spans several organisational levels.

Work-system interaction

SEIPS

AnswersHow did people, tasks, tools, organisation and environment interact to shape work and outcomes?

Switch whenA simple SEIPS scan becomes disconnected “buckets” rather than an interaction analysis.

Functions vary

FRAM

AnswersHow did ordinary variability in connected functions combine to produce an unusual outcome?

Switch whenThe main issue is governance, authority or feedback across levels.

System levels and control

AcciMap or CAST

AnswersHow did decisions, constraints, communication and feedback across the system permit the loss?

Use with careThese methods need skilled facilitation and a clearly defined system boundary.

Many event logs

Process mining

AnswersWhat paths actually occur, where do they diverge and which variants are associated with delay or failure?

LimitA discovered pattern still needs domain interpretation and causal testing.

06 / CORE TOOLKIT

What the common techniques actually produce

Each technique below is useful only when its output changes the next investigative decision.

Reconstruct

Timeline

Combine events and surrounding conditions from several sources using one clock and explicit provenance.

Output
A sequence showing detection, decisions, hand-offs, controls and recovery—not merely a list of timestamps.
Mini example
The booking release went live at 18:00; contact eligibility fell at the next migration job; reminder failures appeared two days later.
Main trap
Filling gaps with a neat narrative. Mark unknowns and contradictions instead.
Drill down

5 Whys

Move from an observed effect toward actionable system conditions by asking what made each step possible.

Output
One or more evidence-linked chains. Branch whenever several conditions are necessary.
Mini example
Reminder not sent → record ineligible → contact flag not mapped → migration test omitted that field.
Main trap
Stopping at “staff did not check”. Ask what made checking difficult and what control should have detected the problem.
Generate then test

Fishbone and evidence matrix

Use domain-appropriate headings to generate hypotheses, then record the evidence for, against and still needed for each one.

Output
A prioritised testing plan—not a diagram presented as proof.
Mini example
People, booking process, technology, access, measurement and environment produce hypotheses about missed appointments.
Main trap
Counting brainstormed ideas as causes or using generic categories that hide interactions.
Compare

Change analysis

List what differs between a reference that worked and the case that failed.

Output
Differences ranked by timing, mechanism and exposure.
Mini example
The definition stayed stable, but contact mapping, lead-time distribution and rescheduling routes changed.
Main trap
Assuming every coincident change is causal. Test which difference can produce the observed pattern.
Study controls

Barrier analysis

Examine controls intended to prevent the event, detect it, limit its consequences or enable recovery.

Output
A barrier table showing intended function, actual performance, evidence and reason for weakness.
Mini example
Reminder validation should detect bad contacts; the failed-message queue should enable recovery; neither worked reliably.
Main trap
Listing “training” as a barrier without checking whether the workflow makes the desired action possible.
Prioritise

Pareto and stratification

Rank categories by frequency, harm or cost, then split the data by meaningful groups, time and process stage.

Output
A focused area for investigation and a warning about who may be hidden by the total.
Mini example
Most additional misses occur in two clinics and among appointments without successful reminders.
Main trap
Frequency is not causality. A small category may also carry the greatest harm.
07 / ACTION

An analysis is only as strong as its corrective action

RCA²—root cause analysis and action—captures the essential point: learning must become sustainable system change.

Weak

Depends on memory

Warnings, policies, reminders, extra checks and retraining. Sometimes necessary; rarely sufficient alone.

Intermediate

Makes success easier

Standardisation, simplification, workflow-integrated checklists, better hand-offs, redundancy and decision support.

Strong

Changes the system

Elimination, substitution, physical or software constraints, forcing functions, monitored automation, fail-safe design and engineered resilience.

Strong is not automatically safe. Automation can create hidden dependence; constraints can block legitimate exceptions. Test actions with the people affected, retain recovery paths and monitor displaced risk.

08 / BEYOND A SINGLE ROOT

RCA is a starting family, not the final frame

Modern practice widens the investigation when linear chains cannot represent interactions, adaptation, governance, repeated process variation or uncertainty about intervention effects.

Work system

SEIPS

Examines how people, tasks, tools and technology, organisation, internal environment and external environment interact to shape processes and outcomes.

Use when: healthcare or service work depends on several interacting conditions. Do not: sort facts into isolated categories and call that systems analysis; the value lies in the relationships.

Across levels

AcciMap

Places evidence across levels such as frontline work, management, organisation, regulators and wider policy, then examines how decisions and pressures connect vertically.

Use when: local action is shaped by governance, contracts, regulation or resource decisions. Output: a multi-level influence map, not one root.

Everyday variability

FRAM

Models the functions required for work and how their inputs, outputs, preconditions, resources, controls and timing vary and combine.

Use when: the same process usually succeeds through adaptation, but ordinary variation can occasionally resonate into failure. Needs: close study of work-as-done.

Control and feedback

CAST

Examines why the safety-control structure did not prevent the loss: responsibilities, constraints, feedback, coordination and decisions at each level.

Use when: safety-critical technology, software and management controls interact. Needs: a defined loss, hazards, system boundary and skilled facilitation.

Repeated pathways

Process mining

Uses timestamped event data from many cases to discover actual paths, check conformance and compare successful and problematic variants.

Use when: the question concerns a recurring process rather than one incident. Limit: event logs show recorded behaviour; they do not supply meaning or causality by themselves.

Intervention effects

Causal inference

Uses explicit assumptions, comparison designs and causal models to estimate whether a factor or intervention changes an outcome beyond ordinary association.

Use when: deciding whether a candidate driver or action has an effect. Limit: missing confounders, changing systems and weak measurement can make a precise estimate wrong.

Future loss

FMEA, bow-tie and STPA

These methods ask how a future process or design could fail. FMEA examines failure modes; bow-tie organises threats and barriers; STPA examines unsafe control actions.

Use when: preventing failure before deployment. They complement learning from incidents but are not retrospective RCA.

Human-supervised

AI-assisted investigation

Models can summarise records, cluster incidents, identify anomalies and propose hypotheses. Their proper role is reducing the search space.

Safeguard: retain provenance, uncertainty and human responsibility. No automated method wins across every system, and fluent output can amplify a false story.

A practical learning progression

Build capability in layers. Later methods do not invalidate earlier ones; they answer questions the simpler lens cannot hold.

  1. Measure the processDefinitions, denominators, variation and data quality.
  2. Reconstruct eventsTimeline, change and barrier analysis.
  3. Map the work systemSEIPS and work-as-done.
  4. Test effectsComparison, causal assumptions and experiments.
  5. Analyse pathwaysProcess mining and recurring variants.
  6. Model complexityFRAM, AcciMap or CAST when justified.
Framework fluency is not investigation competence. A polished diagram cannot replace careful interviewing, reliable records, domain knowledge, causal testing, stakeholder involvement and constructive review by experienced investigators.
09 / PRACTICE

A reusable investigation canvas and quality gate

Use the canvas to brief the work. Use the quality gate before declaring the investigation complete.

  1. Problem statement: expected, actual, where, when, scale, trend and impact.
  2. Immediate response: containment, recovery, temporary controls and evidence preserved.
  3. Scope: governing question, boundary, exclusions, team, stakeholders and response level.
  4. Factual record: timeline, sources, contradictions, unknowns and work-as-done.
  5. Hypotheses: proposed mechanisms, failed or absent controls and evidence required.
  6. Causal claims: evidence for and against, rival explanations, assumptions and confidence.
  7. Learning: what worked, what limited harm and which patterns recur across cases.
  8. Actions: causal link, strength, owner, resources, due date and implementation measure.
  9. Effectiveness: outcome measure, guardrails, exposure period, review date and reviewer.
  10. Reopen rule: the result or unintended effect that sends the team back into investigation.
Evidence quality

Can the claims survive challenge?

  • Facts are separated from inference.
  • Important claims use more than one source where possible.
  • Successful and failed cases are compared.
  • Rival explanations are recorded.
  • Confidence and remaining uncertainty are visible.
Action quality

Can reality disprove the response?

  • Every action targets a supported mechanism.
  • Implementation and effectiveness are measured separately.
  • Affected people helped test the action.
  • Guardrails expose displaced harm or inequity.
  • The review can reopen the analysis.
The investigation is finished only when the action has been tested against reality.Not when the report is signed
10 / SOURCES

Research base and further learning

The sources span healthcare, safety engineering, automotive quality and digital operations. The worked case is fictional; the methodological claims are grounded in the guidance and research below. Accessed 16 July 2026.

  1. NHS England — Patient Safety Incident Response Framework. Proportionate, compassionate and system-based learning from patient safety incidents.
  2. NHS England — Patient safety learning response toolkit. SEIPS, evidence gathering, everyday-work exploration, synthesis and safety-action guidance.
  3. HSSIB — Investigating under PSIRF. Evidence that systems methods require skilled application and should not become superficial categorisation.
  4. HSSIB — The investigator’s toolkit: FRAM. Plain-language explanation of functions, dependencies and variability.
  5. HSSIB — Weight-based medication errors in children. A real investigation combining SEIPS, FRAM, observations, interviews and verification of findings.
  6. National Patient Safety Foundation / AHRQ PSNet — RCA². Emphasises sustainable corrective actions rather than analysis alone.
  7. Google SRE — Postmortem culture: learning from failure. Open, evidence-led postmortems and preventive action.
  8. MIT Partnership for Systems Approaches to Safety and Security — STPA and CAST handbooks. Definitive systems-theoretic guidance for prospective hazard analysis and retrospective accident learning.
  9. International Automotive Task Force — IATF 16949 sanctioned interpretations. Structured problem solving, containment, RCA, systemic corrective action, effectiveness verification and updating process controls.
  10. ISO 31010:2019 — Risk management: risk assessment techniques. International reference covering causal, consequence and prospective risk techniques.
  11. Wang & Qi (2024) — Comprehensive survey on RCA in microservices. Metrics, traces, logs and multimodal automated approaches.
  12. Pham, Ha & Zhang (2024) — Causal inference RCA: how far are we?. Comparative evaluation showing material limitations and no universal winner.
  13. Han et al. (ASE 2024) — LLM and classifier collaboration for failure RCA. Emerging AI-assisted investigation under limited labels.

Interpretive note. “State of the art” varies by domain. Systems methods are established in safety-critical work; process mining, observability and causal or AI-assisted automation depend heavily on data quality and system context. No framework removes the need for judgement, evidence and verification.
