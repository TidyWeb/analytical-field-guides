SWOT Analysis

Weigh up a choice honestly: what is yours to change, and what is not.

This guide is for people meeting the idea for the first time. You don’t need any background. Every new word is explained when it first appears, and one small example runs through the guide.

In short
  1. SWOT sorts what you know into four boxes. Strengths, weaknesses, opportunities and threats. It is a way to look at a decision from all sides before you make it.
  2. The split that matters is inside versus outside. Strengths and weaknesses are about you. Opportunities and threats are about the world around you.
  3. A SWOT is only as good as its evidence. A list of opinions is not an analysis. Each line should be something you can check.
1 · Start here

Weighing up a choice

Say you have been offered a new job. You are not sure whether to take it. So you think about what is good and bad. Some of it is about you: you already know the work, but you have never managed people. Some of it is about the situation: the company is growing fast, but your journey to work gets longer.

That is a SWOT analysis, done on the back of an envelope. The letters stand for the four things you were weighing up.

An everyday picture

Nothing here is clever. The tool just makes you look at all four boxes, so you don’t only think about the ones you like.

S: Strengths

Things you do well, or have going for you. They are inside you or your organisation, so they are yours to build on.

W: Weaknesses

Things you do badly, or lack. Also inside, so also yours to change, if you choose.

O: Opportunities

Chances that come from outside. You didn’t make them, but you could take advantage of them.

T: Threats

Risks that come from outside. You can’t switch them off, but you can get ready for them.

So what is a SWOT analysis?

A SWOT analysis is a way of looking at a decision or plan by sorting what you know into strengths, weaknesses, opportunities and threats. Strengths and weaknesses are inside you. Opportunities and threats are outside.

The method is often credited to Albert Humphrey, who worked at Stanford in the 1960s and 70s. It is now used widely, including in the NHS (the UK’s National Health Service), in councils and in small businesses.

The one test that sorts everything

Beginners often put a point in the wrong box. Two quick questions fix that. First: is it inside us, so we could change it, or outside, so we can’t? Second: does it help us or harm us?

Two questions, four boxes

“The supermarket is opening” is outside and harmful, so it is a threat. “We have only one oven” is inside and harmful, so it is a weakness.

A SWOT is a way to organise evidence, not a strategy

Filling in four boxes does not decide anything. It puts what you know in one place so you can decide. The thinking still has to happen afterwards, and the later sections show how.

2 · Is this the right tool?

When to use SWOT, and when something else fits

SWOT helps you look at one decision or plan from all sides. Check that this is the question you are asking.

1Choose between options

“Should we do this or not?”

This is what SWOT is for. Put the option in the question and weigh it up.

2Plan the year ahead

“Where do we stand, and what should we aim at?”

A SWOT gives you a starting picture. Then choose a few things to act on.

3Check how something is going

“Is this service or project working?”

Use it as a review. Repeat it every so often and see what has moved between boxes.

4Find out why something failed

“A problem happened and we need to know why.”

Use root cause analysis instead. SWOT lists factors. It doesn’t test which one caused a particular failure.

5Explore a big topic

“I don’t know where to begin.”

Draw a mind map first. Then use SWOT once you have an option to weigh up.

6Check a detailed plan for risks

“Could this plan fail, step by step?”

Use a step-by-step failure check. SWOT is too broad for that.

Tip
SWOT works best on one clear question. “How is the business doing?” gives a vague answer. “Should we sell online?” gives a useful one.
3 · The method

The six steps: from a question to an action plan

A good SWOT follows the same shape each time, whether it takes ten minutes or a whole workshop.

The six steps
  1. Set the question. Write down the decision or objective in one sentence. “Should we sell our bread online?” is a good one. “Look at the bakery” is too loose to answer.
  2. Pick the people. Decide who needs to be involved. Include people who know the facts, not just people who have opinions. A SWOT done by a few people in a room can reflect only what they happen to believe.
  3. Gather the evidence. Evidence means facts you can check, such as records, figures and what customers actually said. Mix numbers with other kinds of information, and use the facts to guide your thinking, not opinion alone.
  4. Fill in the four boxes. One point per line. Make each point specific enough that someone else could check it. The next section shows how.
  5. Rank and combine. Decide which points matter most and how sure you are of each. Then pair them up to see what choices they point to.
  6. Act and recheck. Pick a few actions, give each an owner and a date, and agree when you will look again. Circumstances change, so a SWOT is never finished for good.
The steps in order

The yellow steps, 4 and 5, are where the analysis happens. This guide gives you a tool for each of them.

4 · Is the SWOT any good?

How to tell a useful SWOT from a list of opinions

The most common failure is a SWOT full of vague phrases like “strong team” and “good brand”. They feel true, but nobody can check them, and they don’t point to a decision.

Four checks for every line
  1. Is it specific? “Strong reputation” is vague. “About 60 regulars come in every week” is specific.
  2. Can it be checked? Name where the evidence is: till records, a survey, a report.
  3. Is it in the right box? Use the inside-or-outside test from the start of this guide.
  4. Does it matter to this decision? A true point that has nothing to do with the question just adds noise.

From vague to checkable

Here is how a few vague points from our bakery example become useful. The figures are made up.

What SWOT can’t do

Guidance from the NHS and from the CIPD (the professional body for people management in the UK) is open about the limits. Knowing them helps you use the tool well.

It reflects who is in the room

If only a few people take part, the boxes hold their views. Bring in data, and the people closest to the work.

It can stay vague

Short phrases oversimplify. The checks above are your defence.

It is a snapshot

It shows how things look today. Fast change makes the future harder to see, so repeat it regularly.

The boxes blur

The same thing can be a strength in one light and a weakness in another. Decide, and write down why.

Too much or too little data

Too little leaves guesswork. Too much can freeze you. Gather what the decision needs.

It takes time

A useful SWOT needs real evidence-gathering. A five-minute one is fine for a small choice, not a big one.

5 · Our example

Meet the example: a bakery thinking of selling online

The rest of this guide uses one small example. It is made up, so every number is only an illustration.

The situation

A small neighbourhood bakery has four staff and one oven. Customers have started asking whether they can order bread online and have it delivered. The owner is tempted, but worried about taking on too much.

The owner wants to decide in a sensible way, and to explain the decision to the staff.

1The question

“Should we sell our bread online, with local delivery?”

A single question with a yes or no at the end. That makes it a good SWOT question.

2What we want from the analysis
  • All four boxes filled with checkable points.
  • A short list of what matters most.
  • A decision, with a small first step.
6 · The toolkit

Five tools that make a SWOT useful

The grid is the heart of it. The other four help you fill it in well, and turn it into decisions. Each is shown on the bakery example.

Tool 1 of 5 · The SWOT grid

The SWOT grid

Four boxes, one question. The place where everything you know about a decision sits side by side.

1What it’s for

The SWOT grid is a simple two-by-two table. Strengths and weaknesses go along the top, because they are inside. Opportunities and threats go along the bottom, because they are outside.

Use it when you have one clear decision and want to see all sides of it.

2How to do it
  1. Write the question at the top.
  2. Draw four boxes and label them S, W, O and T.
  3. Add one specific point per line, with its evidence.
  4. Move any point that is in the wrong box.
  5. Stop at three or four points per box.
3Shown on our example

Eight points, each one checkable. Notice that the weaknesses (one oven, no web skills) are things the bakery could change, while the threats (supermarket, flour) are not.

4Watch out for
  • Long lists. If a box has ten points, nothing stands out. Keep the ones that matter.
  • Vague phrases. Apply the four checks.
  • One person filling it in alone. Ask the people who know the facts.
Tip
Fill in strengths and opportunities last. People find it easy to list good news and hard to write down the problems.
Tool 2 of 5 · Checkable points

Checkable points

Turn opinions into statements somebody else could confirm.

1What it’s for

A checkable point is a statement that comes with evidence. It says what, how much, and where you looked. It keeps the SWOT honest and stops it turning into a debate about whose view is right.

Use it when a point sounds good but you can’t say where it came from.

2How to do it
  1. Read the point aloud.
  2. Ask: what would we show someone to prove it?
  3. Rewrite it with a number, a date or a source.
  4. If you can’t find any evidence, mark it “to check”.
  5. Keep it only if it matters to the decision.
3Shown on our example
BeforeAfter
“We have a loyal customer base.”About 60 regulars come in every week (till records).
“Delivery would be popular.”Customers keep asking if we deliver. We could count how many asked in a month.

The second row shows an honest answer when you don’t yet have the number: write down how you would get it.

4Watch out for
  • Making up a figure to sound sure. Write “to check” instead.
  • Evidence that is one person’s memory. Note it as an opinion.
  • Spending weeks on evidence for a small point.
Tip
If you can’t say where a point comes from, it is an opinion. That is fine, but label it as one.
Tool 3 of 5 · Ranking

Ranking

Decide which points matter most, and how sure you are of each.

1What it’s for

Not every point counts equally. Ranking means giving each one two scores: how much it matters to the decision, and how sure you are it is true. The pairing shows you where to act and where to find out more.

Use it when your boxes are full and you need to decide what to focus on.

2How to do it
  1. List your points.
  2. Give each a rating for how much it matters: high, medium or low.
  3. Give each a rating for how sure you are: high, medium or low.
  4. Act first on what matters and is solid.
  5. Gather evidence for what matters but is unsure.
3Shown on our example
PointHow much it mattersHow sure are we
About 60 regulars every weekHighHigh: till records
One oven, full 5am to 9amHighHigh: baking rota
New estate has no bakeryHighMedium: we haven’t asked residents
Supermarket opens in springMediumMedium
Flour cost rose last yearMediumHigh: invoices

The estate matters a lot but we aren’t sure there is demand. That is the one to check before spending money.

4Watch out for
  • Ranking by who shouts loudest. Use the two scores.
  • Giving everything “high”. Force some to be medium or low.
  • Ignoring the unsure ones. They are often where the surprise is.
Tip
“Matters a lot, but unsure” is the most useful label in the whole table. It tells you what to find out next.
Tool 4 of 5 · Turning lists into choices

Turning the lists into choices

Pair the four boxes to see what the SWOT is telling you to do. This is often called TOWS.

1What it’s for

TOWS is SWOT turned into action. It was described by Heinz Weihrich in 1982. You pair a strength or weakness with an opportunity or threat, and each pairing suggests a different kind of response.

Use it when your SWOT is done and you ask, “so what do we do?”

2How to do it
  1. Take your top points from each box.
  2. Strengths with opportunities: how can we use what we do well to take the chance?
  3. Strengths with threats: how can what we do well reduce the risk?
  4. Weaknesses with opportunities: can the chance help us fix the gap?
  5. Weaknesses with threats: what must we avoid doing?
3Shown on our example

Four pairings, four different kinds of action. The bottom right is defensive: it is about not making things worse.

4Watch out for
  • Stopping at the SWOT. A list of boxes does not make a decision.
  • Forcing an idea into every pairing. Leave a box empty if nothing fits.
  • Treating every option as equally good. Go back and rank them.
Tip
Start with strengths and opportunities. It is the easiest pairing, and often the best first move.
Tool 5 of 5 · Scanning the outside

Scanning the outside

Six questions that help you find opportunities and threats you would otherwise miss.

1What it’s for

PESTLE is a checklist for the world outside your organisation. The letters stand for political, economic, social, technological, legal and environmental. It is used alongside SWOT to fill the opportunities and threats boxes.

Use it when the outside boxes feel thin, or you are not sure what to look for.

2How to do it
  1. Take each of the six headings in turn.
  2. Ask what is changing under that heading.
  3. Write down anything that could help or harm.
  4. Check each one against evidence.
  5. Add the ones that matter to your SWOT.
3Shown on our example

For the bakery, “economic” prompts the flour cost. “Social” prompts customers’ new habit of ordering online. “Legal” prompts a check on what rules apply to delivering food.

4Watch out for
  • Making a long list for every heading. Look for what matters here.
  • Treating it as a form to fill in. It is a prompt to think.
  • Listing things that never change. Look for movement.
Tip
Pick the two or three headings most likely to matter to your question, and spend your time there.

8 · Common problems

When a SWOT goes wrong

Most poor SWOTs fail in the same few ways.

1A list of opinions

The boxes are full of phrases nobody can check.

Fix: rewrite each point with a number or a source.

2Points in the wrong box

A weakness is listed as a threat, or the other way round.

Fix: ask “inside or outside?” for every point.

3Stopping at the lists

The SWOT is pinned up and nothing changes.

Fix: turn the lists into choices, then give each action an owner and a date.

4Everyone agrees too easily

The group shares one view, and the SWOT hides what it doesn’t know.

Fix: bring in someone outside the team, and look at the data.

5Too many points

Twenty items per box, so nothing stands out.

Fix: rank them and keep the top few.

6Never revisited

The picture was true last year and nobody noticed it change.

Fix: put a review date in the diary when you finish.

9 · Going further

Groups, repeating it, and AI help

These are the main ways people extend a basic SWOT.

In a group

A workshop can bring different views together. Ask people to write their points alone first, then share. That stops the loudest voice setting the picture.

Repeating it

Run it again every six or twelve months, or when something big changes. Compare the new boxes with the old ones to see what moved.

With other tools

Pair it with PESTLE for the outside world, a mind map for brainstorming points, and root cause analysis when a weakness needs digging into.

What about using AI to fill in a SWOT?

An AI tool can draft a SWOT quickly. That can help you get started, or remind you of something you missed.

The risk is that it writes confident, general points that fit any business. It does not know your till records, your oven or your customers. Treat anything it writes as a list of prompts to check against your own evidence, not as findings.

10 · The example, start to finish

The bakery, from question to first step

Here is the whole example in one place. The details are made up.

Points on the grid
8

Two per box, each with a named source.

Matter a lot
3

The regulars, the oven, and the new estate.

Needs checking
1

Whether people on the new estate would actually order.

1Question and people

The owner wrote “Should we sell our bread online, with local delivery?” and asked the baker, the shop assistant and the person who does the accounts.

2Evidence

Till records gave the regulars. The baking rota showed the oven. A look at the council’s pages and a walk to the estate gave the rest.

3The grid

Eight checkable points went into the four boxes. Two vague points (“good brand”, “competition”) were rewritten or dropped.

4Ranking

The estate scored high on importance but only medium on certainty, because nobody had asked the residents.

5Choices

Strengths with opportunities pointed to delivery for the estate. Weaknesses with threats said: don’t promise times that one oven can’t meet.

6First step

Instead of a full launch, the owner chose a six-week trial: Saturday deliveries to the estate, taking orders by phone. Review date set at the end of the six weeks.

The SWOT did not make the decision. It stopped the owner leaping in, or staying out, on instinct. And it turned a vague worry into one specific thing to find out.

11 · Checklist

Before you finish

Ten questions
  1. Question. Is there one clear decision at the top?
  2. People. Did the people who know the facts take part?
  3. Inside or outside. Is every point in the right box?
  4. Specific. Does each point say what and how much?
  5. Checkable. Can you say where the evidence is?
  6. Few. Have you kept to the points that matter?
  7. Ranked. Do you know what matters most, and how sure you are?
  8. Choices. Have you paired the boxes to find actions?
  9. Owners. Does each action have a person and a date?
  10. Review. Have you set a time to look again?
