Analytical Field Guide · plain English

Mind Mapping

Get the whole question on one page, so you can see what you know and what is missing.

This guide is for people meeting the idea for the first time. You don’t need any background, or any software: a pen and a sheet of paper will do. Every new word is explained when it first appears, and one small example runs through the guide.

In short
  1. A mind map is a picture of your thinking. One question sits in the middle. Big themes branch off it, and smaller ideas branch off those.
  2. It is a tool for thinking, not a tool for looking clever. It helps you get ideas out of your head, group them, and spot what is missing.
  3. It is one tool of several. A concept map, an outline and a planning map all do related jobs. This guide shows all four and says when each one fits.
1 · Start here

A list, or a map?

Imagine you are helping to run an open day at a community garden. Ideas arrive in no particular order: posters, a tea urn, who brings chairs, what if it rains. The usual thing is to write them down as a list. A list keeps the order the ideas turned up in, and that order tells you nothing.

A map keeps the same ideas but puts related ones next to each other. You can see at a glance which themes are crowded and which are empty.

The same six ideas, two ways

On the list, “rain?” is just item four. On the map it sits under safety, which makes you ask what else belongs there.

So what is a mind map?

A mind map is a diagram built around one central idea. Main ideas branch off the centre, and smaller ideas branch off those. It is made with short keywords instead of sentences, so you can take in the whole thing at once.

A map is a way to think, not a finished product

The first version of a map is always rough. That is the point. You draw it to find out what you know, what you don’t, and what connects to what. Then you redraw it, or act on it.

If you only ever produce a tidy map, you have probably spent the time on decoration instead of thinking.

2 · Is this the right tool?

When a map helps, and when something else fits

A map is good at one thing: showing how a lot of loose ideas group together. Before you start, check that this is what you need.

1Explore a question

“I have a big topic and don’t know where to begin.”

This is what a map is best at. Put the question in the middle and let the themes appear around it.

2Plan a piece of work

“I need to organise an event, a project or a talk.”

A map shows the parts. Then turn each branch into a short list of actions, with a person and a date.

3Make sense of something you are learning

“I want to see how this chapter fits together.”

Drawing it yourself is the useful part. Copying someone else’s map teaches you much less.

4Find out why something failed

“A problem happened and we need to know why.”

Use root cause analysis instead. A map can help you brainstorm possible causes, but it cannot test them against evidence.

5Weigh up a decision

“Should we do this or not?”

A SWOT analysis fits better. It sorts what you know into strengths, weaknesses, opportunities and threats.

6Record exact steps

“People need to follow a precise order.”

Write a numbered list or draw a flowchart. A map has no order, so it is the wrong shape for a procedure.

Tip
The centre of the map decides the job. “Open day” gives you a plan. “Why did the last one flop?” gives you ideas about causes. Choose the centre on purpose.
3 · The method

The six steps: from one question to a useful map

Every mind map follows the same shape, on paper or on a screen. Start in the middle of the page and work outwards.

The six steps
  1. Write the centre. Put one short question or topic in the middle. A question works better than a single word: “How do we run a good open day?” tells you what the map is for. Draw a shape round it so it stands out.
  2. Add main branches. Draw thick lines out from the centre, one for each big theme. Aim for roughly four to seven. If you have twelve, some of them probably belong together.
  3. Add keywords. On smaller branches, write one or two words per idea. A keyword is a word that carries the idea, so “posters” rather than “we should put up some posters”. Short labels are quick to read and easy to move.
  4. Group and rename. Look at what you have. Move ideas that belong together onto the same branch, and rename any branch whose label no longer fits.
  5. Join across. Draw a few lines between ideas on different branches. These are cross-links. Add a few words to each so you remember why it is there. Only link things that really affect each other.
  6. Check and use. Ask what is missing, what is crowded, and what needs doing next. If you spot a gap, go round again.
The loop

Steps 2 to 5 are where most of the thinking happens. Expect the map to change shape while you work, and treat the first version as a draft.

Paper first

A large sheet turned sideways gives you room in every direction. Start in the middle and keep each branch in its own area.

Colour with a reason

Give each main branch its own colour. Colour is useful for grouping and spotting a branch at a distance. It is not required.

Pictures optional

Small drawings can make a branch easier to find. Many guides recommend them. Use one where it helps you remember, and skip it where it doesn’t.

4 · Is the map any good?

How to tell a good map from a messy one

A map is good if it helps you think or act. Looks are secondary. Here is a quick way to judge your own.

Two maps of the same job

The map on the left has been written like a page of notes. The one on the right says less and shows more.

1Can you scan it?

Look at the map for ten seconds. Can you say what the main themes are? If you are reading sentences, shorten them to keywords.

2Are the branches balanced?

One huge branch and five tiny ones means the big one is really several themes. Split it.

3Does it show a gap?

A useful map makes you say “we haven’t thought about that”. If it only shows what you already knew, push on one branch.

4Can you act on it?

You should be able to turn at least one branch into a next step. If not, the centre question may be too vague.

Does mind mapping actually work?

The honest answer is: it helps some people with some jobs, and the evidence is mixed. It is worth knowing this before you rely on it.

One study

In a 2002 study of 50 medical students, recall improved straight away in both the mind-map group and a group that studied however they liked. A week later, the improvement held up clearly only for the mind-map group. The researchers also noted that motivation in that group was a concern, so the result needs care.

Another study

A later study compared mind maps with ordinary note-taking and found the two groups did about equally well on short-term recall and critical thinking.

A related tool

Concept maps, covered below, have better evidence behind them. Studies that pool many experiments find they tend to help, though results vary a lot. The benefit is larger when learners draw the map themselves.

What to take from this: use a map to explore, organise and plan. Don’t expect it to be a memory trick. You will see claims online such as “mind maps improve learning by a fixed percentage”. Treat any single neat figure with suspicion.

5 · Our example

Meet the example: a garden open day

The rest of this guide uses one small example. It is made up, so the details are only illustrations.

The situation

A community garden wants to hold an open day in May. Visitors will be able to look round, buy plants, and have tea. A handful of volunteers will run it.

Nobody has done this before, so the organiser wants to see everything that has to happen on one page before dividing up the jobs.

1The centre question

“How do we run a good open day?”

Phrased as a question, it keeps the map focused on what the group is trying to do.

2What we want from the map
  • See all the jobs in one place.
  • Spot anything nobody has thought of.
  • Turn the branches into jobs with owners.
6 · The toolkit

Four kinds of map, and what each is for

“Mind map” is often used for anything with boxes and lines. In practice there are four useful kinds. Each gets a short section below, shown on the open day example.

  • Mind mapOne question, many themes.
  • Concept mapHow do these ideas relate?
  • OutlineThe same ideas, as a tidy list.
  • Planning mapBranches into actions and owners.
Tool 1 of 4 · Mind map

Mind map

One central question, with themes branching off it. The quickest way to get a whole topic onto a page.

1What it’s for

A mind map has one centre. Everything else hangs off it in a tree shape, with no labels on the lines. It is for exploring a topic and getting your thoughts out.

Use it when you are starting something new and want to see all the parts.

2How to do it
  1. Write the question in the middle.
  2. Draw one thick branch per theme.
  3. Add keywords on thinner branches.
  4. Add a cross-link where two ideas affect each other.
  5. Step back and look for gaps.
3Shown on our example

Six themes came out of the first ten minutes. The grey line joins “wet-weather plan” to “garden tours”, because rain could cancel the tours. That link would be easy to miss in a list.

4Watch out for
  • Sentences on the branches. Keep to one or two words.
  • Too many branches. More than about seven usually means some should merge.
  • Nothing linking the branches. Without cross-links, a map is little more than a tidy list.
Tip
Let the map grow outwards in every direction. If you start in a corner, you run out of room.
Tool 2 of 4 · Concept map

Concept map

Ideas joined by labelled links, so you can see how they relate and not just that they do.

1What it’s for

A concept map can have several important ideas, not just one centre. Each line carries a few words that say how two ideas connect, such as “run” or “put off”. It is for understanding how things relate.

Use it when you are trying to understand a subject, or explain how its parts affect each other.

2How to do it
  1. List the main ideas, as nouns.
  2. Draw each one in its own box.
  3. Join related boxes with an arrow.
  4. Write a short verb or phrase on every arrow.
  5. Read each arrow aloud as a sentence. If it doesn’t make sense, fix the label.
3Shown on our example

On the left, a mind map says that weather matters. On the right, a concept map says how: bad weather puts visitors off, and visitors are what the stalls depend on.

4Watch out for
  • Unlabelled arrows. Without the words, it is just a mind map with more lines.
  • Too many boxes. Keep to the ideas that matter for your question.
  • Copying someone else’s map and calling it learning. Building your own is where the benefit comes from.
Tip
Test a link by reading it as a sentence: “bad weather puts visitors off”. If it sounds wrong, the label is wrong.
Tool 3 of 4 · Outline

Outline

The same ideas as a tidy indented list. Best when you need to write something or present it in order.

1What it’s for

An outline is a map turned into a list. The branches become headings and the keywords sit underneath. You lose the picture, but you gain an order you can follow when you write.

Use it when you have finished thinking and need to write, present or share in a fixed order.

2How to do it
  1. Start from a finished map.
  2. Turn each main branch into a heading.
  3. List its keywords underneath.
  4. Put the headings in the order your reader needs.
  5. Fill in sentences only now.
3Shown on our example

The open day map, written out. Six headings, eighteen keywords, and a clear place to start. What it cannot show is the link between rain and the tours.

4Watch out for
  • Starting with an outline. It makes you commit to an order before you know what you think.
  • Throwing away the map. Keep it: it shows links the outline hides.
Tip
Map first, outline second. Use the map to find ideas and the outline to deliver them.
Tool 4 of 4 · Planning map

Planning map

Branches turned into actions, each with an owner and a date. This is how a map becomes something that gets done.

1What it’s for

A planning map is a mind map you have gone back over with one extra question: what is the first action on each branch, who does it, and by when? The map shows the whole job and the list gets it done.

Use it when the ideas are in place and you need to share out the work.

2How to do it
  1. Take a finished map.
  2. For each branch, pick the first action.
  3. Give each action one owner.
  4. Give each action a date.
  5. Check that every branch has an owner.
3Shown on our example

Three branches turned into jobs. The names and dates are made up. Notice what the exercise exposes: until someone writes “Tom” beside the wet-weather plan, nobody has actually taken it on.

4Watch out for
  • Actions with no owner. “We should do this” gets done by nobody.
  • Actions with no date. They drift to the bottom of the pile.
  • Trying to plan every branch at once. Start with the ones that block others.
Tip
Look at the branches with no action. They are the gaps the map was meant to expose.
7 · Choosing

Which kind should I draw?

Start from what you are trying to do. Most real jobs use two or three of these in a row.

Pick by the job
If you…Use thisYou get
I have a big topic and don’t know where to start.Mind mapAll the themes, on one page.
I need to understand how ideas affect each other.Concept mapIdeas joined by labelled links.
I’ve finished thinking and need to write or present.OutlineA clear order to follow.
The ideas are there. Now people need jobs.Planning mapActions, owners and dates.

A common sequence is a mind map to explore, a concept map to check how things connect, a planning map to share out the work, and an outline to write it up.

8 · Common problems

When maps go wrong

Most bad maps fail in one of a few ways. Each has a simple fix.

1Writing sentences

The map turns into notes in boxes, and you can no longer scan it.

Fix: cut each label to one or two words. Put the detail in a separate note.

2One giant branch

Everything lands under one heading because it was the first that came to mind.

Fix: split it into two or three themes and give each a name.

3Decoration over thought

Hours go on colours, icons and neat curves, and the map says little.

Fix: keep the first version ugly. Tidy only a map you will reuse.

4Drawing it alone

One person’s map can hide the things only others know about.

Fix: show it to someone else and ask “what’s missing?”

5Never acting on it

The map looks complete and then sits in a drawer.

Fix: turn the branches into a planning map before you close the page.

6Using it for the wrong job

A map cannot prove a cause, or give a step-by-step procedure.

Fix: go back to the table of jobs and choose another tool.

9 · Going further

Paper or screen? Alone or in a group? What about AI?

The method is the same whichever you choose. These are the main differences.

Paper

Quick to start, no menus to learn, and awkward to edit. Good for the first rough version.

Screen

Easy to move branches, share and keep. Good once you know roughly what you are drawing. Many free and paid tools exist.

Group

A shared sheet or screen lets everyone add branches. The quietest person often knows the thing nobody else has thought of, so ask each person in turn.

What about AI-made maps?

Some tools will draw a map from a prompt or from a document. That can be a useful starting point, because it gets something on the page fast.

The trade-off is the one you met in the evidence section: building the map yourself is where much of the benefit seems to come from. If you let a tool do all of it, you get a picture without the thinking. Use a generated map as a first draft to argue with, and then change it.

10 · The example, start to finish

The open day, from first idea to jobs

Here is the whole example in one place. The details are made up.

Branches
6

People, Place, Publicity, Activities, Money, Safety.

Keywords
18

Three on each branch after the first round of grouping.

Gaps found
2

Nobody owned the wet-weather plan, and nobody had set a budget.

1Centre and branches

The organiser wrote “How do we run a good open day?” in the middle and the group called out themes. Six came up in ten minutes.

2Keywords and grouping

Each branch got a few keywords. “Rain?” started loose, and moved under Safety as “wet-weather plan”.

3Cross-link

One link was added: the wet-weather plan affects the garden tours, because rain could cancel them.

4What the map showed

Two branches had no one responsible: Safety and Money. Neither had come up in the first conversation.

5Planning map

The group gave each branch a first action, an owner and a date. Three of them are shown in the planning map earlier in this guide.

6Outline

The organiser wrote the programme and the volunteer briefing from the outline, in the order the visitors would meet things.

The map did not make the decisions. What it did was put everything in one place early, so the missing pieces showed up while there was still time to deal with them.

11 · Checklist

Before you finish

Ten questions
  1. Centre. Is there one clear question in the middle?
  2. Job. Do you know whether you are exploring, studying or planning?
  3. Branches. Do you have roughly four to seven main themes?
  4. Keywords. Are the labels one or two words?
  5. Balance. Is any branch much bigger than the rest?
  6. Links. Does every cross-link carry a few words saying why?
  7. Gaps. Did you look for an empty branch?
  8. Other eyes. Has someone else looked at it?
  9. Action. Does each branch have a first step, an owner and a date?
  10. Kind. Is a map the right tool, or do you need root cause analysis or SWOT?
